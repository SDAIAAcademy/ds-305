Session 1

What a validator needs

  • Name the three kinds of evidence an independent validation must produce
  • Separate a claim the data can test from one it cannot
  • Recompute a headline metric on rows the developers never saw
01
What validation must show

A validation answers three questions, not one

Conceptual soundness

Is the design fit for the purpose: the target, the data, the features, the training choices?

Outcome analysis

Does it perform on rows it never saw: discrimination, calibration, intervals, every group?

Monitoring

Will anyone notice when it stops working: which metric, which threshold, who acts?

This week. Day 1 and 2 are outcome analysis; Day 3 tests soundness of the explanations; Day 4 adds monitoring and the decision.

The three-part frame comes from model risk practice in banking supervision (SR 11-7 of the US Federal Reserve calls them conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring and outcomes analysis). It is not Saudi regulation; it is a widely used vocabulary, and the capstone report is organised on it. Ask the room which of the three their own organisation does least. Usually monitoring.

Sources: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, SR 11-7 (2011).

Stress the rows. The 50,000 applications are split once: 30,000 train for the developers, 10,000 calib (where the validator fits any repair, such as a recalibration map or a conformal threshold) and 10,000 holdout (where the validator judges). Nothing is ever fitted on the holdout.

Reading a model card

Every claim gets one of three verdicts

Supported

The package's evidence, or your recomputation, backs the claim as written.

Unsupported

The evidence is missing, or it contradicts the claim. Missing counts.

Untestable

No data you have, or could have today, can test it as written. Rewrite it as a test.

The habit. "Supported" needs evidence. Silence is not support.

Walk through the vocabulary before the lab. An untestable claim is not necessarily false; it is a promise. The validator's move is to turn it into a monitoring condition with a metric, a threshold, data and an action. That is step 5 of Lab 1.1.

Responsible use

Validation data is applicants' personal data, even in a model risk team

  • Income, loans and missed payments of named people fall under the Personal Data Protection Law.
  • A validator needs outcomes and predictions, not names: ask for the minimum, and ask the data protection officer.
  • Subgroup columns (age band, gender, region) are needed to audit the model and must not leak into it.

In this course

Every applicant is synthetic. The capstone uses the course packages or a peer's synthetic package; no real borrower data enters a repository.

Keep it practical: the habit is to ask Sadeem, the data protection officer, what the validation extract may contain and how long it is kept. The course does not teach the law. Its official address is on the course page.

02
Two different properties

A model can rank applicants well and still state the wrong risk

0.755
AUC on the holdout: who is riskier than whom
0.40
mean predicted default probability, against 0.10 observed
  • Discrimination: do defaulters get higher scores? AUC answers it, and ignores the scale.
  • Calibration: when the model says 20%, do 20% default? AUC is blind to it.
  • Officers read the score as a probability, so both matter.

Numbers from Lab 1.1. Multiply every score by four and the AUC does not change at all. Ask: who at Mizan uses the probability itself, not the rank? Pricing (a risk premium), provisions for expected loss, and the officer who reads "40%" and declines. All three are harmed by a fourfold overstatement.

Scoring a probability

A proper score rewards the forecaster for saying what they believe

def brier(y, p):
    return np.mean((p - y) ** 2)

def log_loss(y, p):
    p = np.clip(p, 1e-12, 1 - 1e-12)
    return -np.mean(y * np.log(p)
                    + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))
  • Both are proper: the expected score is best when the forecast equals the true probability.
  • Accuracy at 0.5 is not: 0.51 and 0.99 score the same.
  • Log loss punishes a confident miss hard; Brier is bounded and easier to explain.

Do the toy case on the board: outcomes 0, 1, 1 with forecasts 0.2, 0.7, 0.9 give Brier 0.0467 and log loss 0.228 (the lab's first check). Then ask what happens to the log loss if the first forecast were 0.999: it explodes. That is why the lab clips.

Sources: Gneiting and Raftery, Strictly Proper Scoring Rules (2007).

Recalibration

A recalibration map sits after the model and changes only the meaning of the score

Amanascore, unchanged
→
Mapfitted on the calib rows
→
Probabilityjudged on the holdout
  • The map is monotone, so the ranking and the AUC do not move.
  • The developers can adopt it without retraining: it is a recommendation with evidence.

Emphasise the three sets of rows: training rows (never used for the map, because the model is overconfident there), calib rows (fit the map), holdout (judge it). The same three-way split returns on Day 2 for conformal thresholds.

Two methods

Platt scaling fits one smooth curve; isotonic regression can step anywhere

Platt scaling

A logistic regression of the outcome on the logit of the score: two parameters, a smooth S-shaped map. Steady with few rows.

Isotonic regression

Any non-decreasing step function of the score. Flexible, needs many rows, can overfit a small calib set.

lr = LogisticRegression(C=1e6).fit(logit(p_cal).reshape(-1, 1), y_cal)
platt = lambda q: lr.predict_proba(logit(q).reshape(-1, 1))[:, 1]

Platt is the logistic model from DS-301 with one feature. Ask why it uses the logit of the score rather than the score: on the logit scale a constant shift is exactly what rebalancing did to the odds, so Platt can undo it.

Sources: scikit-learn user guide, probability calibration.

The limit of the repair

A map of the score cannot see what the score cannot see

  • It fixes the average relation between score and outcome.
  • It cannot fix a group whose risk differs at the same score: a column the model never had.
  • It cannot improve the ranking.
  • It holds while new applicants resemble the calib rows; a shift breaks it again.
Next session. Amana excludes region and age band on purpose. Is it calibrated within them?

04
Calibration by subgroup

Calibrated on average can hide a group the model under-predicts

  • After Platt, mean predicted 0.090 against 0.099 observed on the holdout.
  • Region and age band are not features of Amana, by design.
  • If risk differs between them at the same score, the average hides it.

Why it matters at Mizan

An under-predicted group gets loans priced below its risk; an over-predicted one is declined more than it should be. Both are harms to people.

Excluding a column is a choice about inputs. It does not make the outcome independent of that column. Ask: does the exclusion protect the young applicant or hide a problem with how the model treats them? This is the start of the fairness thread that Day 4 tags and DS-309 takes further.

Noise in small groups

A gap in a group of 500 needs an interval before it is a finding

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    ph = k / n
    c = (ph + z*z/(2*n)) / (1 + z*z/n)
    h = z * np.sqrt(ph*(1-ph)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / (1 + z*z/n)
    return c - h, c + h
  • The Northern region has 508 holdout applicants; 78 defaulted.
  • The 95% Wilson interval for its rate: 12.5% to 18.8%.
  • A finding is a prediction outside the interval, not any gap.

The Wilson interval behaves better than the plus-or-minus formula for small groups and rates near zero. A group with 50 rows would have an interval of about plus or minus ten points: almost nothing could be called a finding. Say so in the report rather than drawing conclusions.
